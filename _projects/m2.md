Low-power transmitter and receiver architectures such as backscatter and injection-locked tunnel-diode radios reach microwatt budgets by delegating carrier generation to an external emitter device. This third device complicates deployment. A two-device link on a microwatt budget was considered impossible: stable microwave oscillators draw milliwatts, and microwatt tunnel-diode oscillators drift too much for communication.

{% include project_figures.liquid images="m2_stability.png|780" caption="Frequency stability of the M² oscillator over 14 hours. The 434 MHz carrier drifts by less than 1 kHz while the oscillator draws 105 µW." %}

M² breaks this trade-off. Its core is an oscillator that couples a tunnel diode with a surface acoustic wave resonator, a sharply tuned mechanical element, and holds its frequency within 2.10 parts per million over hours, at least two orders of magnitude better than tunnel-diode oscillators. The same circuit also shifts data onto and off the carrier, so it serves as both transmitter and receiver. It draws roughly 105 µW while streaming data to commodity receivers or to another M² node in a symmetric link, without any external carrier source, and its transmitter reaches 135 m in line of sight.

M² was published at ACM MobiSys '26 as a Featured Paper (top 10 of 302), and its poster won Best Poster Runner-Up. It was demonstrated at ACM MobiCom '26 and received a Best Demo Honorable Mention at the ACM/IEEE ISLPED '26 Low-power Design Contest.
